\vspace{4mm}
\begin{fullwidth}
{\fontsize{12}{15}\selectfont\bfseries\color{sicblue}Abstract}\par\vspace{0.5mm}
{\small
\textbf{Bayan} is an Android app that rewrites a selection of Arabic text, from
inside any app and entirely on the phone, to lower its \emph{linguistic load}:
shorter clauses and fewer hard words. Most of the work went into evaluation. On
SAMER, the main human-written Arabic simplification corpus, copying the input
scores 77.5 SARI, so we built \textbf{BayanBench} (1,981 items), which scores
meaning with an open model that agrees with human raters (AUC \hAucSame{}) and
simplification, restraint and correctness with code. Our best model, \vThree{},
decoded with four beams, outperforms every earlier model: it keeps the meaning in
\mBeamTKept\% of test outputs (earlier models at most \bvHmaxKept\%) and cuts
\mBeamTClause{} words from the longest clause, and raters found the rewrites it made
easier \hmThreeChEasier\% of the time. It is the app's large model, at 471\,MB and
about 1.2\,s per sentence on a mid-range phone.\par}
\end{fullwidth}

\section*{Bayan at a Glance}
\label{sec:glance}

\lead{The problem.} Long clauses, rare words and missing short vowels make Arabic
text hard for readers with dyslexia, and the tools that simplify text send it to a
server.

\lead{What we built.} Bayan is an Android app. A reader selects Arabic text in any
app and chooses \ar{تبسيط} (\emph{Simplify}); a simpler version appears over the page,
written entirely on the phone and offline. Bayan can also add tashkeel (the short
vowels) and read the text aloud.

\lead{The models.} Released on Hugging Face (\texttt{huggingface.co/Congi-libya}) as \textbf{BayanSimplify}: \vOne{} (AraT5v2 on
SAMER), \vTwo{} and its compact \vTwoFast{} (AraT5v2 and AraBART on a 22,733-pair mix),
and \vThree{} (AraT5v2 on our own corpus, the best). The app downloads \vTwoFast{} by
default and offers \vThree{} as its large model.

\lead{What it achieves.}

% ============================================================== 1 Introduction
\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Background Information}
\label{sec:background}

Developmental dyslexia affects an estimated 11\% of Arab primary-school children,
pooled across 18 studies ($N$ = 30,243) \cite{aldakhil}. It impairs reading
through phonological processing, and Modern Standard Arabic adds obstacles of its
own. Short vowels are normally left unwritten, so one written form carries
several readings: \ar{كتب} is \emph{kataba} (he wrote), \emph{kutiba} (it was
written) or \emph{kutub} (books), and the reader must resolve it from context.
Clitics attach directly to the stem, producing long words that resist chunking,
and Arabic-speaking children with reading disabilities show early deficits in
exactly this morphological layer \cite{saieghtaha}. Prose chains clauses with
\ar{و} and \ar{ف} across whole paragraphs, so one sentence can exceed working
memory.

The evidence on what helps is uneven, and we build on the parts that have been
measured. More frequent and shorter words sped reading and improved
comprehension for readers with dyslexia \cite{rello2013,rivero2021}. Unpacking
bound morphemes into separate words helped Arabic readers with reading
disabilities and not their typical peers \cite{asadi2024}. Texts simplified by
splitting clauses into short sentences were read faster and understood better by
beginning readers, most by the weakest of them \cite{javourey2022}. Full
diacritization is contested: it adds visual crowding \cite{hermena2015}, and its
benefit for our readers has not been tested. We therefore treat \emph{simplify
first} as the core intervention and diacritization as an optional stage.

\lead{Related work.} SAMER \cite{samer} is the main
human-written Arabic simplification corpus: 15 novels rewritten at two easier
levels, sentence by sentence. BAREC \cite{barec} labels 69,441 sentences with 19
reading levels but is not parallel; LLM-written \cite{baseet} and human-verified
\cite{daasi} corpora add other domains. None was written for readers with
dyslexia, and none teaches sentence splitting (\autoref{sec:eda}). Control tokens
let one model rewrite for different audiences \cite{scarton2018,access,muss}, and
our strength tags follow that line. SARI \cite{sari}, the field's standard score,
correlates only $r$ = 0.36 with human judgements of simplicity
\cite{alvamanchego2021} and misses inserted or substituted content
\cite{devaraj2022}; reference-free reading-level estimates \cite{cripwell2023} and
edit-level error taxonomies \cite{salsa} track simplicity and meaning better. Our
benchmark follows behavioural testing \cite{checklist}, construct-validity and
reporting checklists \cite{bean2025,betterbench} and document-clustered intervals
\cite{miller2024}. LLM judges favour text close to what they wrote
\cite{li2026}, so we check ours against people, with agreement measured by
Krippendorff's $\alpha$ \cite{krippendorff}.

\subsection{Motivation and Objective}
\label{sec:objective}

Readers meet difficult Arabic inside other applications --- a message, a news
page, a PDF --- not in a reading app. Bayan therefore appears where the text
already is: selecting text on an Android phone offers \ar{تبسيط}
(\emph{Simplify}) in the system selection menu, and the rewrite opens in a panel
over the same app. Every model runs on the phone, so the reader's text never
leaves it and the tool works offline.

The data for this did not exist. SAMER is small, literary and teaches mostly word
substitution; the other public sources are LLM-written or cover one domain.
Dyslexia-targeted neural simplification exists for English \cite{madjidi2023},
and the one Arabic dyslexia study with a language model used it as a tutor, not
as a rewriter \cite{gharaibeh2025}. Our searches of 17 and 23 September found no
system that combines Arabic, automatic rewriting to lower reading difficulty, and
on-device inference. Three research questions organise this report:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*}, leftmargin=10mm]
\item Does the standard reference-based evaluation measure what this task needs,
      and what does a model learn from the main human-written corpus?
\item Can a model lower the linguistic load of Arabic text (long clauses, hard
      words) without changing its meaning, and how much of that can we show with
      validated measures?
\item Does it run on a mid-range phone at interactive latency, and what do
      quantisation and the deployed input format cost?
\end{enumerate}

\noindent We set four objectives, assessed in \autoref{sec:accomplish}:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{O\arabic*}, leftmargin=10mm]
\item A simplifier that beats copying the input on held-out sentences a human
      simplified, and that lowers linguistic load without losing meaning.
\item Diacritics restored on the simplified text without altering its letters.
\item The pipeline running on a mid-range Android phone at interactive speed,
      with a default simplifier within 250\,MB.
\item Evidence from readers with dyslexia, collected with informed consent.
\end{enumerate}

\lead{Scope of the claim.} Bayan addresses readability simplification and
read-aloud, in any app. Dyslexia has other axes that we have not tested with
readers: decoding (the app can add tashkeel, a setting off by default), visual
layout (the app has typography settings), the auditory route, and fluency. No
reader with dyslexia has used the app itself yet; one has rated its rewrites. We
therefore claim a measured reduction of linguistic load and the means to measure
it, not that Bayan helps readers with dyslexia.

\lead{Contributions.} (1) An evaluation audit: copy-heavy test sets make SARI
reward inaction, and 9.3\% of a public Arabic simplification corpus overlaps the
SAMER and BAREC test sets. (2) BayanBench, a benchmark of behaviours with an open
meaning scorer checked against human raters. (3) A small meaning judge that
catches planted errors at 30 times its teacher's speed. (4) \vThree{}, a model
that keeps meaning more often than every earlier one while simplifying more,
running on the phone inside an Android app.

\lead{The synthetic corpus, v0.} Our first pipeline drew five DeepSeek V4.1 Flash
candidates per BAREC train or dev source and accepted one only if an LLM
equivalence validator and the CAMeL BAREC readability model both passed it. Its
audit found tashkeel in 56.5\% of rows, 63.4\% of cut-off sources ``completed'',
and a validator that accepted 34\% of planted added sentences, so we never
trained on it and kept it online, tagged \texttt{v0}, with these defects stated.

\lead{The synthetic corpus, v1.} Corpus v1 replaces it. Gemma~4 31B writes the
targets from a stricter prompt, a calibrated Qwen judge checks meaning at a
threshold of 0.75, and a target must lead its source by at least two readability
levels. The 14,975 rows have no violation of the rule checks, and an audit of
1,039 pairs found 1.7\% with a changed meaning. It trains \vThree{}.

\subsection{Training Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\lead{Task and base models.} Given a source sentence $x$ and a strength tag $t$,
the model produces a rewrite $\hat y = f(t \oplus x)$ that keeps the meaning of
$x$ and is easier to read; training minimises token-level cross-entropy. We
considered three pretrained encoder--decoders (\autoref{tab:basemodels}): AraT5v2-base-1024 \cite{arat5,octopus,t5}
(368M parameters, 46\% of them in two untied embedding matrices), AraBART
\cite{arabart,bart} (139M) and an Arabic HPLT T5 \cite{nort5,hplt}. AraT5v2 came
first because it beat AraBART at all three levels in the published Baseet
baselines \cite{baseet}; AraBART, 2.6 times smaller, is the on-device candidate.
Neither vocabulary was built for diacritized text (1.6\% of AraT5v2's pieces carry
a diacritic, and AraBART maps every mark to an unknown token), so every model
receives its source with tashkeel and tatweel removed, in training and in the app.

\lead{\vOne{}.} AraT5v2 fine-tuned to rewrite SAMER's level-5 sentences into
their level-3 simplifications, with the prefix \ar{بسّط:}\ (\emph{simplify:}):
AdamW at $10^{-4}$, effective batch 16, 10 epochs, seed 42; unchanged pairs (42\%
of SAMER train) capped at 20\%, and the checkpoint with the best SARI on a 500-row
dev subset kept.

\lead{\vTwo{} and \vTwoFast{}.} The diagnosis of \vOne{} (\autoref{sec:modeling})
found three problems, and the v0.2 recipe changes one thing for each:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item \emph{Inaction was rewarded for the wrong reason.} All 6,033 unchanged SAMER
      pairs are kept, so the model learns when not to edit, and the checkpoint is
      selected on SARI over dev rows a human changed.
\item \emph{SAMER is novels, and teaches word substitution.} DAASI's everyday and
      administrative text and Baseet's educational text are added. Baseet's
      targets are LLM-written and often summarise, so a pair is kept only if it
      passes a \emph{meaning filter} (LaBSE similarity $\geq$ 0.646, at least
      67\% of the source's length, every number kept), each threshold the 5th
      percentile of the same measure over DAASI's human-verified rewrites.
\item \emph{One strength does not fit every reader.} Every source carries a tag
      naming the style it was learned from (\autoref{tab:m2data}), so at run time
      one model serves light, medium and strong rewrites \cite{scarton2018,access}.
\end{enumerate}

Both architectures were trained with one shared script, so the architecture is
the only difference: 10 epochs, batch 8 with 2 accumulation steps, fp16, seed 42,
learning rate $10^{-4}$ (AraT5v2) or $5\cdot10^{-5}$ (AraBART, whose prefix
\ar{بسط:}\ drops the shadda its tokenizer cannot read). The script disables
length-grouped batching, which in \texttt{transformers}~4.46 also shuffles the
evaluation set, and stops if predictions and references fall out of order.

decodes with four beams (\autoref{sec:modeling}). \autoref{fig:evolution} shows how
each model's measured problem shaped the next.

\lead{Evaluation: BayanBench.} SARI measures agreement with one
editor's rewrite. A reader needs something else: a selection that comes back
\emph{easier} and \emph{with the same meaning}, and text that should not change
left alone. No public benchmark tests this for Arabic, so we built one against the
checklists of \cite{bean2025,betterbench}. Every output is scored on separate
measures, never combined into one number (\autoref{tab:tracks}):
\emph{meaning kept}, an output whose P(same meaning) from the scorer below is at
least 0.5 and that keeps every number of its source; \emph{simpler}, as words cut
from the longest clause (sources with a clause over 15 words), reading levels
lowered (CAMeL BAREC readability model) and hard words removed (SAMER lexicon
levels 4--5); \emph{behaviour}, as easy text left unchanged, protected text
keeping its letters and a cut-off selection keeping its ending; and \emph{flags}
for a changed count of negations, limits or conditions. A clause is a span between
punctuation marks, so the clause measure is always read next to meaning.

The 1,981 items form four sets reported side by side: \emph{core} (BAREC test

every corpus we use) and \emph{written} (50 medicine-leaflet sentences). Items are
split by document (731 dev items from 129 documents, 1,250 test items from 197),
and any BAREC document with a sentence in any training data was dropped whole.
Meaning is scored by Gemma~4 31B \cite{gemma4} in vLLM \cite{vllm}, as a scorer
rather than a writer: it sees the source and the output, never the system's name,
and the probability of \emph{yes} to four questions (same meaning; a fact added;
a fact dropped; a contradiction) is read from one forward pass. It replaces the
closed Gemini~3.1~Pro judge of the first version, because anyone can rerun it and
its graded score can be checked against people (about \$0.12 for 2,700 pairs); the
0.5 threshold was set before the human ratings and kept after them. Scores are
shared data keyed by the (source, output) pair, an unscored output is reported as
pending rather than guessed, and the v0.2 training data contains nothing a Gemma
model wrote or approved, which limits preference leakage \cite{li2026}. Every rate carries a 95\% interval from a
bootstrap over whole documents (2,000 resamples) \cite{miller2024}, and a
comparison counts only when the interval of the paired difference excludes zero.
The benchmark ships as a Python package with its data and scores in a
checksum-pinned Hugging Face dataset, so anyone on the team can rerun it.

\lead{Human rating protocol.} To check the scorer, raters saw
\hSingles{} single outputs and \hPairs{} comparisons from the six v0.2 systems on
the dev split (\hDocs{} documents), blind to the system. For each output they
answered \emph{meaning} (same, a minor change, a major change), \emph{Arabic}
(correct, a minor or a major error) and \emph{ease} for a reader with dyslexia
(easier, the same, harder); for a comparison, which version was better for such a
reader. \hEven{} outputs were drawn evenly per system and \hNear{} where the
scorer was least sure; \hShared{} went to every rater, to measure agreement. Five
team members rated on a web page built for the round. A sixth rater, a reader with
dyslexia, gave informed consent and answered only the ease question, worded for
them as \mbox{\ar{هل كانت أسهل عليك؟}} (was it easier for you?). An output's
meaning counts as kept when a strict majority of its raters said \emph{same}.

\lead{The meaning judge.} For screening data and checkpoints we
distilled the 31B scorer into a four-head Gemma~4 E2B judge, released on the Hub
in three variants (bf16, w8a8 and w4a16). At a 5\% false-reject rate it flags
95.5\% of planted errors and 86.5\% of changes humans labelled as meaning changes,
about 94\% of the teacher's score at about 30 times the speed. The 31B model
remains the reference scorer in BayanBench.

\subsection{Workflow}

Each task is a GitHub issue with an owner, a reviewer and a definition of done;
changes arrive as reviewed pull requests, with merged work from every member. SAMER
text and model outputs on SAMER move only through private Kaggle datasets and a
private Hugging Face organisation, never through the repository.
\autoref{fig:pipeline} shows the data path and its three gates. The leakage check
compares every training file with both locked test sets, by exact match and by
character 5-gram Jaccard overlap of 0.8 or more; the meaning filter admits
LLM-written pairs only if they keep the source's meaning; generated pairs pass
validators before release. The locked test sets are pinned by row count and
SHA-256 and re-verified before every test run.

\subsection{System Design}
\label{sec:design}

\autoref{fig:arch} shows the system. The Android app registers an activity for
\texttt{android.\allowbreak intent.\allowbreak action.\allowbreak PROCESS\_TEXT},
so \ar{تبسيط} appears in the selection menu of any app, and a translucent activity
draws the result over the host app; an optional floating panel keeps reading while
the reader returns to the page.

\lead{Inference.} Each simplifier ships as an int8 ONNX bundle (encoder, decoder
with a key--value cache, and tokenizer), downloaded once, verified by SHA-256 and
run by ONNX Runtime with a decoding loop written in Kotlin. The fast model and
\vTwo{} decode greedily and stream each token as it is produced. \vThree{}, the
large model, decodes with four beams (the setting \emph{More faithful, slower}, on
by default; \#61, \#62, \#66) and shows each sentence when it is final: a port of
transformers' beam search that gives transformers' exact tokens on 120 of 120
BayanBench sentences. The app strips tashkeel, splits the selection into sentences
and decodes them one by one; sentences under six words (headings, labels) pass
through unchanged.

\lead{The app's text step.} Before the model, the selection is cleaned (PDF line
breaks, presentation forms, list bullets), digits are mapped to Western digits,
and scripture and set poetry are detected and \emph{never sent to the model}.
After it, a sentence keeps its source wording when the rewrite keeps less than
35\% of its content words, drops or changes a number or a Latin-script word, or
changes the count of negations, limits (\emph{at least}, \emph{only}) or condition
words. On the BayanBench dev core items these guards raise numbers kept from 72\%
to 100\% and protected text and cut-off endings to 100\%, remove every flagged
negation and limit change, and raise meaning kept by about 3.5 points; they fall
back on 3--7\% of sentences. The original is always one tap away.

\lead{Read-aloud and diacritization.} Voices run on the device through
sherpa-onnx; the default, Nabra-7M-Distill, is an 8.7\,MB int8 Arabic voice, and
the spoken word is highlighted. The diacritizer, Libtashkeel
(\autoref{tab:diac}), is a 4.8\,MB character model bundled with the app. With
\emph{Show tashkeel} on (off by default, since full vowels can crowd a line), each
finished sentence gets its short vowels after the guards; the Kotlin port gives
the Python package's exact output on 305 BayanBench texts and never changes a
letter.

Four properties of the corpora (\autoref{tab:eda}, \autoref{fig:lengths})
explain the behaviour of the models:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{SAMER carries no diacritics}, while 54\% of BAREC test does, so a
      model trained only on SAMER has never seen a diacritized input.
\item \textbf{SAMER simplifies words, not sentences.} Its median length ratio is
      exactly 1.00: the editors substituted vocabulary and kept structure.
\item \textbf{No corpus teaches splitting.} A sentence is added in 0\% of SAMER
      pairs, 2--4\% of Baseet's and 8\% of DAASI's, so existing data cannot teach
      the restructuring the reading research supports most \cite{javourey2022}.
\item \textbf{Half of SAMER needs no change.} 42--49\% of pairs are identical,
      and SAMER splits by chapter, so its test set is in-domain: both make SARI on
      SAMER read optimistically.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Modeling}
\label{sec:modeling}

\lead{RQ1: the reference-based view.} Reference-based scores use SARI \cite{sari}
beside BLEU, BERTScore \cite{bertscore} and the fall in predicted BAREC level,
always paired with the copy baseline and split into rows a human changed and rows
a human left unchanged. Because half of SAMER needs no change, copying scores
SARI \copytestall{} on the full test set but only \copytestchanged{} on the
\testchangedn{} rows a human changed, and the second number is the bar a model
must clear (\autoref{tab:test}, \autoref{fig:sari}).

\finding{\textbf{\vOne{} simplifies where it should and over-edits where it should
not.} On the \testchangedn{} test rows a human simplified, greedy decoding scores
\greedychanged{} against the copy baseline's \copytestchanged{} (\greedygain{} SARI).
On the \testunchangedn{} rows a human left alone it scores \greedyunchanged{}
against a perfect 100, because it edits \greedyoveredit\% of sentences that needed
no change. The two effects net to \greedyallgap{} overall, so the headline number
would report a working model as worse than doing nothing.}

\lead{What \vOne{} learned.} It returns \greedycopy\% of test rows unchanged where
the editors left \humancopy\%, and when it edits, \greedysingle\% of its edits
change one word (editors: \humansingle\%): it substitutes vocabulary and never
restructures (\autoref{fig:behaviour}a). On BAREC's \diacn{} test sentences that carry tashkeel, raw input
keeps only \diacrawkept\% of the source words and \diacrawlost\% of outputs lose
more than half the sentence; with tashkeel stripped, \diacstrippedkept\% and
\diacstrippedlost\% (\autoref{fig:behaviour}b). On DAASI's administrative text it scores \daasisari{} against
a copy baseline of \daasicopysari{} (\daasigain{}). Some of its substitutions
reverse the meaning: \ar{عزلة} (isolation) became \ar{استعباد} (enslavement). SARI
is blind to this class of error, which is why BayanBench scores meaning
separately.

\subsection{Testing and Improvements}

\lead{Testing.} The first plugin shell shipped 41 unit and Robolectric tests; the
app unit-tests its text pipeline, and its download pipeline checks every file by
SHA-256. The leakage checker is tested against a planted duplicate. The benchmark
package has 12 tests, and its prediction command reproduces our v0.2 outputs on
all 731 dev items, as an independent rerun by a teammate confirmed. Running the
pipeline on real data exposed four harness faults: every BAREC row scored as
``unchanged''; \vOne{}'s training prefix missing from the shared prediction script
(twelve SARI points); length-grouped batching shuffling the evaluation set; and
garbled outputs under \texttt{transformers}~5. Each now has a guard.

\textbf{RQ1:} no. On copy-heavy test sets SARI rewards inaction and is blind to
reversals of meaning; an open meaning scorer, by contrast, agrees with human raters
(AUC \hAucSame{}) and can be rerun by anyone. \textbf{RQ2:} partly. Every v0.2
setting traded meaning for simplification; \vThree{} with four beams moves the
trade-off, keeping the meaning more often than any earlier model while
simplifying more than \vTwo{} in the app. \textbf{RQ3:} yes. Streamed output
reaches the reader in under a third of a second with either bundle, \vThree{} with
four beams shows a sentence in about 1.2\,s, and the default fits the 250\,MB
budget; the large model needs 471\,MB.

\lead{Benefits.} For readers: a simpler version where and when the difficulty
occurs, offline and private, with the original one tap away. For schools: a tool
that runs on the phones students already own. For the field: a documented case of
test-set contamination in a public Arabic simplification corpus, an evaluation
protocol that exposes copy-heavy scores, and a benchmark, a meaning judge and four
public models.

\subsection{Future Improvements}
\label{sec:future}

The central next step is to simplify as much as the boldest setting while keeping
meaning as well as the safest. The meaning scorer, now checked against people, can
rank candidate rewrites and select checkpoints, so that training is steered by the
behaviours readers need rather than by SARI. Faster beam decoding would let
\vThree{}'s quality arrive word by word; pruning the AraT5v2 vocabulary (46\% of its
parameters sit in two embedding matrices) could bring its 471\,MB bundle to about
223\,MB, if the full evaluation shows no loss; and
sentence splitting needs training data that teaches it. Above all, a reading study
with more readers with dyslexia, measuring reading time and comprehension, would
turn ratings into evidence about reading.

\lead{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{One reader with dyslexia}, who rated rewrites on a web page; nobody
      with dyslexia has yet used the app, and the team raters do not have dyslexia.
\item \textbf{The meaning scorer is a model}, stricter than people (it fails
      \hWrongFail{} of the \hKept{} outputs they kept) and checked on dev outputs of
      the v0.2 systems.
\item \textbf{Ease and Arabic are weakly agreed} between raters ($\alpha$
      \hAlphaEase{} and \hAlphaArabic{}), so ease is measured through clause
      length, reading level and hard words, and the clause measure credits added
      commas as well as splits.
\item \textbf{\vThree{}'s meaning rests on the judge.} No significant difference is
      not proof of none: its interval against \vTwo{} on test runs from
      \mBeamTKeptCi{} points, and its human round covers 18 sentences.
\item \textbf{Some tracks are small.} E3 and E4 hold 64 and 40 test items, and few
      core items contain a number, so these rates separate only large differences.
\item \textbf{No reference-based test score for the v0.2 models}: their test
      evaluation is BayanBench, which has no human references.
\item \textbf{One run per configuration, one phone.} Each model was trained once,
      with one seed, so the comparison between architectures carries no estimate of
      training variance; on-device figures come from one mid-range phone.
\end{itemize}

\lead{Conclusion.} Bayan shows that Arabic text can be simplified entirely on a
mid-range phone, from inside any app, at interactive latency, and that the
reduction in linguistic load can be measured honestly. Getting there depended less
on model size than on evaluation: the field's standard score hid both what a model
had learned and what it broke, and the behaviours a reader needs had to be measured
one by one before they could be improved. Measured that way, \vThree{} with four
beams is the best model we built, and it is the one the app ships.
