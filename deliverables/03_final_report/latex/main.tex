\vspace{2pt}\textbf{\color{sicblue}Abstract.}\enspace
Arabic poses problems for readers with dyslexia that tools built for Latin
scripts do not address: short vowels go unwritten, clitics fuse into long words,
and clauses chain across whole paragraphs. We present \textbf{Bayan}, an Android
system that rewrites a selection of Arabic text, from inside any application and
entirely on the phone, into a form that is easier to decode. We first show that
the standard evaluation misleads for this task: on SAMER, the only human-written
Arabic simplification corpus, copying the input scores 77.5 SARI, and a
fine-tuned AraT5v2 that beats copying on the sentences a human changed still
loses to it overall. We then train two encoder--decoder models, AraT5v2 (368M
parameters) and AraBART (139M), on a 22,733-pair corpus in which every unchanged
pair is kept, LLM-written pairs enter only through a meaning filter derived from
human rewrites, and a strength tag conditions each source. Building that corpus
exposed that 9.3\% of a public Arabic simplification dataset overlaps the
standard test sets. To measure what a reader needs rather than agreement with one
editor, we introduce \textbf{BayanBench}, 1,981 items in 13 behavioural tracks
and four item sets, split by document and scored per measure with
cluster-bootstrap intervals. On its test split, the medium strength setting
removes every clause over 15 words in \btTfiveB--\btBartB\% of long-clause items and
reduces hard words in \btTfiveHard--\btBartHard\% of items, while keeping at least 60\% of
the source's words in \btBartKept--\btTfiveKept\%. It also over-edits easy text, rewrites
protected scripture, and completes cut-off selections, failures that a
reference-based score does not surface. Quantised to int8, the AraBART bundle
takes 222\,MB; the larger AraT5v2 bundle, run on a Snapdragon 870 phone, shows
the first word of a rewrite 177--318\,ms after the request, and of each later
sentence within 85\,ms.

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Background Information}
\label{sec:background}

Developmental dyslexia affects an estimated 11\% of Arab primary-school children,
pooled across 18 studies ($N$ = 30,243) \cite{aldakhil}. It impairs reading
through phonological processing, and Modern Standard Arabic adds obstacles of its
own. Short vowels are normally left unwritten, so one written form carries
several readings: \ar{كتب} is \emph{kataba} (he wrote), \emph{kutiba} (it was
written) or \emph{kutub} (books), and the reader must resolve it from context.
Clitics attach directly to the stem, producing long words that resist chunking,
and Arabic-speaking children with reading disabilities show early deficits in
exactly this morphological layer \cite{saieghtaha}. Prose chains clauses with
\ar{و} and \ar{ف} across whole paragraphs, so one sentence can exceed working
memory.

The evidence on what helps is uneven, and we build on the parts that have been
measured. More frequent and shorter words sped reading and improved
comprehension for readers with dyslexia in eye-tracking studies
\cite{rello2013,rivero2021}. Unpacking bound morphemes into separate words helped
Arabic readers with reading disabilities and not their typical peers, the one
Arabic finding with a tested group interaction \cite{asadi2024}. Texts
simplified by rules that split subordinate and coordinated clauses into short
sentences were read faster and understood better by beginning readers, and most
by the weakest of them \cite{javourey2022}. Full diacritization, by contrast, is
contested: it adds visual crowding \cite{hermena2015}, and its benefit for our
readers has not been tested. We therefore treat \emph{simplify first} as the core
intervention and diacritization as an optional later stage.

\subsection{Motivation and Objective}
\label{sec:objective}

Readers meet difficult Arabic inside other applications --- a message, a news
page, a PDF --- not in a reading app. Bayan therefore appears where the text
already is: selecting text on an Android phone offers \ar{تبسيط}
(\emph{Simplify}) in the system selection menu, and the rewrite opens in a panel
over the same app. Every model runs on the phone, so the reader's text never
leaves it and the tool works offline.

The closest prior systems leave this setting open. Dyslexia-targeted neural
simplification exists for English \cite{madjidi2023}; Arabic simplification
corpora and models exist without a dyslexia target \cite{samer,baseet}; and the
one Arabic dyslexia study with a language model used it as a tutor, not as an
automatic rewriter \cite{gharaibeh2025}. Our literature searches of 17 and 23
September found no system that combines the three: Arabic, a dyslexia-oriented
rewrite, and on-device inference. Building one raises three questions, which organise this
report:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*}, leftmargin=10mm]
\item Does the standard reference-based evaluation measure what this task needs,
      and what does a model learn from the only human-written corpus?
\item Can one model, trained on meaning-filtered mixed-domain data with a strength
      tag, make Arabic text measurably easier to decode without losing its
      content?
\item Does it run on a mid-range phone at interactive latency, and what do
      quantisation and the deployed input format cost?
\end{enumerate}

We set four objectives against which \autoref{sec:accomplish} reports the
outcome:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{O\arabic*}, leftmargin=10mm]
\item A simplifier that beats copying the input on held-out sentences a human
      simplified, and that makes text measurably easier without losing its
      meaning.
\item Diacritics restored on the simplified text without altering its letters.
\item The pipeline running on a mid-range Android phone, within 250\,MB for the
      simplifier.
\item Evidence from readers with dyslexia, collected with informed consent.
\end{enumerate}

\lead{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{An evaluation audit.} We show that copy-heavy test sets make SARI
      reward inaction, report every score beside a copy baseline and split by
      whether a human changed the sentence, and document that 9.3\% of a public
      Arabic simplification corpus overlaps the SAMER and BAREC test sets
      (\autoref{sec:prep}, \autoref{sec:modeling}).
\item \textbf{A training recipe for one model at several strengths}, built from
      that audit: all unchanged pairs kept, LLM-written pairs admitted by a
      meaning filter calibrated on human rewrites, and a strength tag that becomes
      the reader's setting (\autoref{sec:method}).
\item \textbf{BayanBench}, a behavioural benchmark for dyslexia-oriented Arabic
      rewriting with deterministic and judge tiers, document-level splits,
      cluster-bootstrap intervals, and a judge-acceptance protocol fixed before
      any judge was tested (\autoref{sec:bench}).
\item \textbf{An on-device system}, with int8 encoder--decoder bundles decoded
      token by token inside an Android app and measured on a phone
      (\autoref{sec:design}, \autoref{sec:ondevice}).
\end{itemize}

\autoref{sec:related} places the work in the literature. Section~2 describes the
data, the models, the system and the benchmark; Section~3 reports the results for
each research question; Section~4 draws the conclusions and states the
limitations.

\subsection{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\lead{Arabic simplification data.} SAMER \cite{samer} is the only human-written
multi-level Arabic simplification corpus: 15 novels rewritten at two easier
levels, each version keeping the original's sentences by design. BAREC
\cite{barec} labels 69,441 sentences with 19 human reading levels but is not
parallel. Baseet \cite{baseet} provides three LLM-written levels per source, and
DAASI \cite{daasi} human-verified rewrites of government and insurance text. No
corpus targets readers with dyslexia, and none teaches sentence splitting: a
sentence is added in 0\% of SAMER pairs, 2--4\% of Baseet's and 8\% of DAASI's
(\autoref{sec:eda}).

\lead{Controllable simplification.} A control token prepended to the source lets
one model rewrite for different audiences, first with target-grade tokens
\cite{scarton2018} and later with measured attributes such as length and lexical
complexity \cite{access,muss}. Our strength tags follow this line with one
difference: each tag names the corpus style it was learned from, so that a
meaning filter can be applied to exactly the sources that need one.

\lead{Evaluating simplification.} SARI \cite{sari} is the field's standard, but it
correlates only $r$ = 0.36 with human judgements of simplicity
\cite{alvamanchego2021}, and similarity-based metrics detect deleted content but
not inserted or substituted content \cite{devaraj2022}. Reference-free estimates
of the reading level track simplicity better \cite{cripwell2023}, and edit-level
error taxonomies track meaning \cite{salsa}. For benchmark design we follow
behavioural testing \cite{checklist}, construct-validity and reporting checklists
\cite{bean2025,betterbench}, and document-clustered intervals \cite{miller2024}.
LLM judges are known to favour outputs close to data they wrote or approved
\cite{li2026}, which shapes how we restrict ours.

\lead{The synthetic corpus.} Our generation pipeline takes BAREC train and dev
sources, draws five candidates per source from DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, and accepts
one only if an LLM equivalence validator and the CAMeL BAREC readability model
both pass it. The validator is a DSPy program optimised with MIPROv2 and reaches
84\% accuracy on 25 held-out annotated pairs at the chosen threshold. The export
is split by a salted hash of the normalised source, so that a sentence repeated
under several BAREC identifiers never straddles two splits; a classifier trained
to tell the splits apart scores AUC 0.51. BAREC test was never used. The corpus
was completed after model~2 had been trained, so no result in this report depends
on it.

\subsection{Training Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\lead{Task.} Given a source sentence $x$ and a strength tag $t$, the model
produces a rewrite $\hat y = f(t \oplus x)$ that should keep the meaning of $x$ and
be easier to decode. Training minimises token-level cross-entropy on pairs
$(t \oplus x, y)$; decoding is greedy, the setting the phone runs.

\lead{Base models.} Simplification is a sequence-to-sequence task, so every
candidate is a pretrained encoder--decoder transformer (\autoref{tab:basemodels}).
AraT5v2 came first because it beat AraBART at all three levels in Baseet's
head-to-head evaluation; AraBART is the on-device candidate because it is 2.6
times smaller.

\lead{Input normalisation.} No vocabulary in \autoref{tab:basemodels} was built
for diacritized text: in AraT5v2 only 1.6\% of pieces carry a diacritic, and
AraBART cannot represent one at all. Every model therefore receives its source
with tashkeel and tatweel removed, in training and in the app; \autoref{sec:modeling}
measures what happens otherwise.

\lead{Model~1.} We fine-tuned AraT5v2 to rewrite SAMER's level-5 sentences into
their level-3 simplifications with the prefix \ar{بسّط:}\ (\emph{simplify:}):
AdamW at learning rate $10^{-4}$ with weight decay 0.01 and 100 warm-up steps, an
effective batch of 16, mixed precision, 10 epochs (3,250 steps) and seed 42.
Pairs whose simplification equals the source (42\% of SAMER train) were capped at
20\% of training, and the checkpoint with the best SARI on a 500-row dev subset
was kept.

\lead{Model~2.} The diagnosis of model~1 (\autoref{sec:modeling}) identified three
problems, and the model-2 recipe changes one thing for each:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item \emph{Inaction was rewarded for the wrong reason.} Capping unchanged pairs
      at 20\% while choosing the checkpoint on a dev sample that is 43\% unchanged
      pulls the model both ways. Model~2 keeps all 6,033 unchanged SAMER pairs,
      so it learns when not to edit, and selects its checkpoint on SARI over dev
      rows a human changed.
\item \emph{SAMER is novels, and it teaches word substitution.} Model~2 adds
      DAASI's everyday and administrative text and Baseet's educational text.
      Baseet's targets are LLM-written, and many are summaries that drop facts,
      delete answer options or invent a speaker. A Baseet pair is kept only if it
      passes a \emph{meaning filter}: LaBSE similarity $\geq$ 0.646, a target at
      least 67\% of the source's length, and every number kept. Each threshold is
      the 5th percentile of the same measure over DAASI's human-verified
      rewrites, so a pair is rejected only when it departs from its source more
      than 95\% of human simplifications do.
\item \emph{One strength does not fit every reader.} Every source carries a tag
      naming the style it was learned from (\autoref{tab:m2data}). At run time the
      tag is the reader's strength setting, so one model serves light, medium and
      strong rewrites \cite{scarton2018,access}.
\end{enumerate}

\lead{Model-2 training.} Both architectures were trained on the same data with
one shared script, so that the architecture is the only difference: 10 epochs,
batch 8 with 2 gradient-accumulation steps, fp16, maximum length 256, seed 42,
evaluation twice per epoch with patience 3, and the checkpoint chosen on SARI over
the 600 changed rows of a fixed 900-row SAMER dev sample. AraT5v2 used learning
rate $10^{-4}$ and the prefix \ar{بسّط:}; AraBART used $5\cdot10^{-5}$ and \ar{بسط:}\
without the shadda, which its tokenizer maps to an unknown token. The script
disables length-grouped batching, which in the pinned \texttt{transformers}~4.46
also shuffles the evaluation set and would score each prediction against another
row's reference; it stops if predictions and references ever fall out of order,
and the logs show full alignment at all 40 evaluations.

\subsection{System Design}
\label{sec:design}

\autoref{fig:arch} shows the system as tested on a phone. The Android app
registers an activity for \texttt{android.\allowbreak intent.\allowbreak action.\allowbreak PROCESS\_TEXT}
(\texttt{text/plain}, Android 6.0 and later), so \ar{تبسيط} appears in the
selection menu of any app. A translucent activity draws the result over the host
app without the overlay permission; an optional floating panel, which does
require it, keeps reading while the reader returns to the page.

\lead{Inference.} The simplifier ships as an int8 ONNX bundle (encoder, decoder
with a key--value cache, and tokenizer), downloaded once, verified by SHA-256, and
run by ONNX Runtime with a greedy decoding loop written in Kotlin that blocks
repeated 3-grams and stops at 256 tokens. The app strips tashkeel, splits the
selection into sentences and decodes them one by one, streaming each token to the
screen as it is produced. Sentences under six words (headings, labels) pass
through unchanged, because the model was trained on longer sentences and invents
text around fragments. Each output then passes a \emph{retention fallback}: when a
rewrite keeps less than 35\% of the source's words, the source sentence is shown
instead. The original is always one tap away.

\lead{Read-aloud and diacritization.} Voices run on the device through
sherpa-onnx. The default, Nabra-7M-Distill, is a Kokoro-architecture Arabic
voice exported to int8 (8.7\,MB); three Piper voices are optional downloads. The
spoken word is highlighted as it is read. The diacritizer, Libtashkeel, was
selected on the benchmark in \autoref{tab:diac} but is not part of the build we
tested.

\subsection{BayanBench}
\label{sec:bench}

SARI measures agreement with one editor's rewrite. A reader with dyslexia needs
something else: that a selection comes back \emph{easier to decode} and \emph{with
the same meaning}, and that what should not change does not. No public benchmark
tests this for Arabic, so we built one against the construct-validity checklist
of \cite{bean2025} and the reporting practices of \cite{betterbench}.

\lead{Construct.} A rewrite is dyslexia-friendly when it says the same thing in a
form that is easier to decode and follow: no clause over 15 words (BAREC's
guideline limit for level 10, about grade 4), more familiar words, no new errors,
and restraint where the text is already easy. Visual formatting, read-aloud and
tashkeel are out of scope, and summarising is always a failure: dropping content
never counts as simplification.

\lead{Tracks and measures.} Each track targets one behaviour and is scored on its
own (\autoref{tab:tracks}); the probe tracks follow CheckList \cite{checklist}. A
\emph{clause} is a maximal span between punctuation marks (full stop, question and
exclamation marks, comma, semicolon, colon, line break), so the clause measure
credits added commas as well as split sentences and must be read next to the
content measures. \emph{Kept words} is the share of the source's words of three
or more letters, compared after removing clitics, that reappear in the output.
\emph{Hard words} are words at levels 4--5 of the SAMER readability lexicon, and
\emph{reading level} is the maximum sentence level predicted by the CAMeL BAREC
readability model, counted only for sources at level 10 or above. Numbers are
compared as multisets after mapping Arabic-Indic and Persian digits to Western
ones; negations are counted over eleven negating particles.

\lead{Items and splits.} The 1,981 items form four sets that are reported side by
side and never averaged. \emph{Core} items come from BAREC test documents.
\emph{Held-out} items are news and legal text, including 247 BAREC sentences of
those domains that no model trained on. \emph{Outside} items are 344 sentences
from UN News, WHO fact sheets, newly created Arabic Wikipedia articles, UNHCR
help pages and Android help, fetched on 28 September and therefore published
after every corpus we use. \emph{Written} items are 50 medicine-leaflet sentences
written for the benchmark by an LLM, kept out of core because they are not real
text. Items are split by document (731 dev items from 129 documents, 1,250 test
items from 197), so no document contributes to both splits. Any BAREC document
with a sentence found in any training or generated data was dropped whole (17
documents), and near-copies were removed by word-trigram overlap.

\lead{Two tiers.} The \emph{code tier} is deterministic: every measure above,
plus house punctuation rules, unchanged easy text and protected letters. The
\emph{judge tier} holds meaning and grammar verdicts from Gemini~3.1~Pro with a
fixed Arabic checklist; the judge sees only the selection and the output, never
the system's name. Verdicts are shared data keyed by the content of the
(selection, output) pair, so scoring never calls a judge, an unjudged output is
reported as pending rather than guessed, and a judge-tier rate is shown only once
90\% of its items have verdicts.

\lead{Validating the judge.} We planted seven kinds of error (a changed number, a
dropped negation, a swapped quantifier, a deleted clause, an invented detail, a
mid-sentence full stop, broken agreement) in outputs the judge had passed. It
flagged a major error in all 47, named the planted type in 45, and gave the same
verdict on 12 of 13 pairs judged twice. Any other judge is accepted only if its
verdicts on a 792-pair validation set clear thresholds fixed before any candidate
was tested: recall of meaning errors $\geq$ 0.80, pass rate on clean pairs $\geq$
0.70, $\kappa$ $\geq$ 0.60 against the reference verdicts, planted errors $\geq$
90\% and recall of language errors $\geq$ 0.60. Model~2's training data contains
nothing the judge wrote or approved, and the benchmark's written items come from a
different model family, which limits preference leakage \cite{li2026}.

\lead{Statistics and release.} Every rate carries a 95\% interval from a
bootstrap over whole documents (2,000 resamples), because sentences from one
document pass or fail together \cite{miller2024}; a comparison counts only when
the interval of the paired difference excludes zero. The benchmark ships as a
Python package whose command-line tool covers the whole cycle (\texttt{verify}, \texttt{predict}, \texttt{score},
\texttt{pending}, \texttt{judge}, \texttt{validate-judge}) with its data in a checksum-pinned Hugging Face dataset, so any
team member can score a model on a laptop, on Kaggle or on Linux.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}
\label{sec:prep}

\lead{Conversion.} SAMER's aligned levels were converted to source--target pairs
(level~5 $\rightarrow$ level~3). One converter turns each locked test file into
the scoring format, keeps every row (including the 1,599 SAMER test rows, 48.8\%,
where the two levels are identical) and checks the row count before writing.

\lead{Normalisation for comparison.} For leakage checks and scoring alignment,
diacritics and punctuation are removed and the alef, \ar{ة}/\ar{ه} and
\ar{ى}/\ar{ي} variants are unified. Training text keeps its spelling apart from
tashkeel and tatweel.

\lead{Leakage.} Every training file was checked against both locked test sets and
found clean. Matches under five words are reported but not counted, because short
formulaic fragments recur in any corpus; BAREC alone repeats 311 sentences
between its own train and test splits.

\finding{\textbf{Contamination in a public corpus.} 3,190 of Baseet's 34,294 rows
(9.3\%) overlapped our locked test sets: 1,829 SAMER test sentences and 1,361
BAREC test sentences. Seen from the other side, 47\% of SAMER's substantive test
sentences appear somewhere in Baseet. Training on it unfiltered would have
inflated every later score with no visible error. The rows were removed and the
remaining 31,104 re-checked: zero matches.}

\lead{Meaning filter.} Applied to the Baseet candidates, the filter keeps 6,888
pairs across the three levels. Its purpose shows at the strongest level: the 5th
percentiles of Baseet's level-1 targets are 0.44 LaBSE similarity and 26\% of the
source's length, and 10\% of those targets drop a number, against 0.646, 67\% and
none for DAASI's human rewrites.

Four properties of the corpora (\autoref{tab:eda}, \autoref{fig:lengths})
explain the behaviour of both models:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{SAMER carries no diacritics}, while 54\% of BAREC test and 36\% of
      Baseet do. A model trained only on SAMER has never seen a diacritized input.
\item \textbf{SAMER simplifies words, not sentences.} Its median length ratio is
      exactly 1.00: the editors substituted vocabulary and kept structure.
      Baseet's strongest level shortens a 14-word median source to 9 words.
\item \textbf{No corpus teaches splitting.} A sentence is added in 0\% of SAMER
      pairs, 2--4\% of Baseet's and 8\% of DAASI's, and word length and clitic
      load barely change in any of them. Existing data can teach shorter, more
      common words; it cannot teach the restructuring the reading research
      supports most directly \cite{javourey2022}.
\item \textbf{Half of SAMER needs no change.} 42--49\% of pairs are identical, and
      SAMER splits by chapter rather than by novel, so its test set is in-domain.
      Both make SARI on SAMER read optimistically.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Modeling}
\label{sec:modeling}

\lead{Setup.} Reference-based scores use SARI \cite{sari} through the
\texttt{evaluate} implementation, beside BLEU, BERTScore between source and output
\cite{bertscore}, and the fall in predicted BAREC level. Every score is paired
with the copy baseline and split into rows a human changed and rows a human left
unchanged. Each final model was run once per test set after training and
selection were complete.

\lead{RQ1: the reference-based view.} Because half of SAMER needs no change,
copying scores SARI \copytestall{} on the full test set but only \copytestchanged{}
on the \testchangedn{} rows a human changed, and the second number is the bar a
model must clear. \autoref{tab:test} and \autoref{fig:sari} report model~1, with
the harness validated first: it reproduced the training run's 2,983 dev
predictions row for row.

\finding{\textbf{Model~1 simplifies where it should and over-edits where it should

against a perfect 100, because it edits \greedyoveredit\% of sentences that needed
no change. The two effects net to \greedyallgap{} overall, so the headline number
would report a working model as worse than doing nothing. Beam search changes
little (\beamchanged{} on changed rows).}

\lead{What model~1 learned.} The model returns \greedycopy\% of test rows unchanged

word (editors: \humansingle\%), and its median output-to-input length ratio is

(\autoref{fig:behaviour}a). \autoref{fig:training} shows the role of checkpoint
selection: the SARI that chose the checkpoint had been flat within noise since

\subsection{Testing and Improvements}

\lead{Testing.} The first plugin shell shipped 41 unit and Robolectric tests. The
app tested on the phone unit-tests its text pipeline (sentence splitting,
punctuation repair, clitic normalisation and the retention fallback), and its
download pipeline checks every file by SHA-256. The leakage checker is tested against a
planted duplicate and must exit non-zero. The benchmark package has its own tests
on a built-in toy dataset, and its prediction command reproduces our model-2
outputs on all 731 dev items. Running the pipeline end to end on real data, not
fixtures, exposed three harness faults: the scoring script put every BAREC row in
the ``unchanged'' bucket; the shared prediction script omitted model~1's training
prefix, which alone cost twelve SARI points; and length-grouped batching shuffled
the evaluation set. Each now has a guard.

The answers to the research questions are these. \textbf{RQ1:} no. On copy-heavy
test sets, SARI rewards inaction and is blind to reversals of meaning; a model
that beats copying on the sentences editors changed still scores below copying
overall, and what it learned from SAMER is vocabulary substitution without
restructuring. \textbf{RQ2:} largely yes. One tagged model makes long-clause
sentences easier in about half of the cases and reduces hard words in over 40\%,
keeps most content, and still helps, though less, on text published after its training data; it lacks restraint. \textbf{RQ3:} yes. The int8 AraBART bundle fits the
budget, streamed output reaches the reader in under a third of a second on a
mid-range phone, and quantisation costs AraBART little. The shipped bundles are conservative mainly because they send no strength tag, and for AraT5v2 also because of quantisation.

\lead{Benefits.} For readers: help where and when the difficulty occurs, offline
and private, with the original always one tap away. For schools: a tool that runs
on the phones students already own. For the field: a documented case of test-set
contamination in a public Arabic simplification corpus, an evaluation protocol
that exposes copy-heavy scores, and a benchmark that measures what an Arabic
reader with dyslexia needs rather than agreement with one editor.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Future Improvements}
\label{sec:future}

The evaluation points to specific next steps. Restraint and protection belong in
the app as much as in the model: scripture and poetry should be detected and
never sent to it, a cut-off selection should keep its ending, and Persian-range
digits should be mapped before decoding and restored after. The strength tag
should travel with every request. Sentence splitting needs training data that
teaches it, which no human-written corpus provides. Choosing checkpoints on
BayanBench rather than on SARI would let the behaviours readers need shape
training itself. Last, the benchmark's human track and a reading study with
readers with dyslexia are what would turn these automatic results into evidence
about reading.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{No reader has used Bayan yet.} Every result is automatic. The
      measures are chosen from reading research, but none is validated as a
      predictor of reading success for Arabic readers with dyslexia, and no such
      metric exists in any language.
\item \textbf{Code tier only.} Meaning is checked here through numbers,
      negations and kept words. These catch deletions but not substitutions such
      as the reversal in \autoref{tab:errors}, which only the judge tier detects.
\item \textbf{The clause measure credits commas.} A rewrite can meet the 15-word
      limit by adding commas as well as by splitting sentences. We read it next to
      kept words and reading level, and the 15-word limit is itself a design
      choice: the ranking of the five systems in \autoref{tab:benchtest} is the same at 12, 15 and 20 words, but the absolute rates move.
\item \textbf{No reference-based test score for model~2.} Model~2 is compared
      with published work only through its dev selection score; its test
      evaluation is BayanBench, which has no human references.
\item \textbf{Some tracks are small.} E3 and E4 hold 64 and 40 test items and
      separate only large differences.
\item \textbf{One phone and one bundle.} On-device figures come from one
      mid-range phone and the AraT5v2 bundle.
\item \textbf{One run per configuration.} Each model was trained once, with one
      seed, so the comparison between architectures carries no estimate of
      training variance.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Conclusion}

Bayan shows that an Arabic reading aid for readers with dyslexia can run entirely
on a mid-range phone, from inside any app, at interactive latency. Getting there
depended less on model size than on evaluation. The field's standard score hid
both what a model had learned and what it broke. A public corpus overlapped the
test sets. And the behaviours a reader needs --- shorter clauses, familiar words,
the same meaning, and restraint where the text is already easy --- had to be
measured one by one before they could be improved. BayanBench makes those
measurements reproducible by anyone on the team, and its first results mark
restraint as the problem to solve next.
