\vspace{2pt}\textbf{\color{sicblue}Abstract.}\enspace
Arabic text can be hard to read for many reasons: clauses chain across whole
paragraphs, clitics fuse into long words, and short vowels go unwritten. We
present \textbf{Bayan}, an Android system that rewrites a selection of Arabic
text, from inside any application and entirely on the phone, to lower its
\emph{linguistic load}: shorter clauses and fewer hard words. This is general
readability, not a solution for dyslexia, whose other axes (decoding, visual
layout, the auditory route, fluency) we do not address or have not tested. Most
of the work went into evaluation. On SAMER, the main human-written Arabic
simplification corpus, copying the input scores 77.5 SARI, and a fine-tuned
AraT5v2 that beats copying on the sentences a human changed still loses to it
overall; building our training mix exposed that 9.3\% of a public Arabic
simplification dataset overlaps the standard test sets. We introduce
\textbf{BayanBench} (1,981 items split by document), which scores meaning with an
open model (Gemma~4 31B) and simplification, restraint and correctness with code.
Five raters and a reader with dyslexia rated \hTasks{} outputs and comparisons:
the meaning scorer separates the outputs they judged faithful from the rest with
an AUC of \hAucSame{}, and only meaning was rated consistently enough to validate
a judge, so the benchmark keeps the measures that track people. On it, models
trained on a 22,733-pair mix lower linguistic load at a cost to meaning: the
medium strength setting of AraT5v2 cuts \bvHTfiveClause{} words from the longest
clause and keeps the meaning in \bvHTfiveKept\% of outputs. A second corpus written
by Gemma~4 31B (14,975 rows) trains a third model that, decoded with four beams,
outperforms every earlier model: on the test split it keeps the meaning in
\mBeamTKept\% of outputs (earlier models at most \bvHmaxKept\%) and cuts \mBeamTClause{}
words from the longest clause (\vTwo{} in the app \bvHAppTfiveClause{}); even against \vTwo{}
behind the app's newest checks it is no worse on meaning and cuts about three times as
many words. \vThree{} with four beams is the app's large model. A small meaning judge catches 95.5\% of planted errors. On a
Snapdragon 870 phone the first word of a rewrite appears 82--172\,ms after the
request with the int8 AraBART bundle (222\,MB, the default) and 177--318\,ms with
the optional AraT5v2 bundles (471\,MB).\vspace{2pt}}}
\end{fullwidth}

\section*{Bayan at a Glance}
\label{sec:glance}

\lead{The problem.} Long clauses, rare words and missing short vowels make Arabic
text hard for readers with dyslexia, and the tools that simplify text send it to a
server.

\lead{What we built.} Bayan is an Android app. A reader selects Arabic text in any
app and chooses \ar{تبسيط} (\emph{Simplify}); a simpler version appears over the page,
written entirely on the phone and offline. Bayan can also add tashkeel (the short
vowels) and read the text aloud.

\lead{The models.} Released on Hugging Face (\texttt{huggingface.co/Congi-libya}) as \textbf{BayanSimplify}: \vOne{} (AraT5v2 on
SAMER), \vTwo{} and its compact \vTwoFast{} (AraT5v2 and AraBART on a 22,733-pair mix),
and \vThree{} (AraT5v2 on our own corpus, the best). The app downloads \vTwoFast{} by
default and offers \vThree{} as its large model.

\lead{What it achieves.}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Background Information}
\label{sec:background}

Developmental dyslexia affects an estimated 11\% of Arab primary-school children,
pooled across 18 studies ($N$ = 30,243) \cite{aldakhil}. It impairs reading
through phonological processing, and Modern Standard Arabic adds obstacles of its
own. Short vowels are normally left unwritten, so one written form carries
several readings: \ar{كتب} is \emph{kataba} (he wrote), \emph{kutiba} (it was
written) or \emph{kutub} (books), and the reader must resolve it from context.
Clitics attach directly to the stem, producing long words that resist chunking,
and Arabic-speaking children with reading disabilities show early deficits in
exactly this morphological layer \cite{saieghtaha}. Prose chains clauses with
\ar{و} and \ar{ف} across whole paragraphs, so one sentence can exceed working
memory.

The evidence on what helps is uneven, and we build on the parts that have been
measured. More frequent and shorter words sped reading and improved
comprehension for readers with dyslexia in eye-tracking studies
\cite{rello2013,rivero2021}. Unpacking bound morphemes into separate words helped
Arabic readers with reading disabilities and not their typical peers, the one
Arabic finding with a tested group interaction \cite{asadi2024}. Texts
simplified by rules that split subordinate and coordinated clauses into short
sentences were read faster and understood better by beginning readers, and most
by the weakest of them \cite{javourey2022}. Full diacritization, by contrast, is
contested: it adds visual crowding \cite{hermena2015}, and its benefit for our
readers has not been tested. We therefore treat \emph{simplify first} as the core
intervention and diacritization as an optional later stage.

\subsection{Motivation and Objective}
\label{sec:objective}

Readers meet difficult Arabic inside other applications --- a message, a news
page, a PDF --- not in a reading app. Bayan therefore appears where the text
already is: selecting text on an Android phone offers \ar{تبسيط}
(\emph{Simplify}) in the system selection menu, and the rewrite opens in a panel
over the same app. Every model runs on the phone, so the reader's text never
leaves it and the tool works offline.

Arabic simplification data exists, but it was not enough for this task. SAMER
\cite{samer}, the main human-written corpus, is small and narrow: it is made of
novels and teaches mostly word substitution. The other public sources are
LLM-written or cover one domain. None of them was written for readers with
dyslexia, and neither is our own corpus: we have no sentences written
specifically for them. Dyslexia-targeted neural simplification exists for English
\cite{madjidi2023}, and the one Arabic dyslexia study with a language model used
it as a tutor, not as an automatic rewriter \cite{gharaibeh2025}. Our literature
searches of 17 and 23 September found no system that combines Arabic, automatic
rewriting to lower reading difficulty, and on-device inference. We also chose not
to target every difficulty that dyslexia brings; the scope paragraph below says
which ones we address. Building the system raises three questions, which organise
this report:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*}, leftmargin=10mm]
\item Does the standard reference-based evaluation measure what this task needs,
      and what does a model learn from the main human-written corpus?
\item Can one model, trained on mixed-domain data with a strength tag, lower the
      linguistic load of Arabic text (long clauses, hard words) without changing
      its meaning, and how much of that can we show with validated measures?
\item Does it run on a mid-range phone at interactive latency, and what do
      quantisation and the deployed input format cost?
\end{enumerate}

We set four objectives against which \autoref{sec:accomplish} reports the
outcome:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{O\arabic*}, leftmargin=10mm]
\item A simplifier that beats copying the input on held-out sentences a human
      simplified, and that lowers linguistic load without losing meaning.
\item Diacritics restored on the simplified text without altering its letters.
\item The pipeline running on a mid-range Android phone at interactive speed,
      with a default simplifier within 250\,MB.
\item Evidence from readers with dyslexia, collected with informed consent.
\end{enumerate}

\lead{Scope of the claim.} Bayan addresses two things: readability
simplification, which lowers linguistic load in the form of long clauses and hard
words, and read-aloud (text-to-speech), and it delivers both in any app. Dyslexia has other
axes that this report does not address or has not tested. \emph{Decoding}: short
vowels are unwritten, we strip tashkeel before the model sees the text, and the
app can add it back (a setting, off by default) but we have not tested that with
readers. \emph{Visual layout}: the
app has typography settings, which we have not tested with readers. \emph{The
auditory route}: the app reads aloud, which we have not tested. \emph{Fluency and
working memory}: not measured. No reader with dyslexia has used the app itself yet; one
has rated its rewrites, so objective~O4 has started. We therefore make no claim that Bayan helps readers with dyslexia; what
we claim is a measured reduction of linguistic load and the means to measure it.

\lead{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{An evaluation audit.} We show that copy-heavy test sets make SARI
      reward inaction, report every score beside a copy baseline and split by
      whether a human changed the sentence, and document that 9.3\% of a public
      Arabic simplification corpus overlaps the SAMER and BAREC test sets
      (\autoref{sec:prep}, \autoref{sec:modeling}).
\item \textbf{A benchmark, and a check on its own measures.} BayanBench scores
      behaviours rather than agreement with one editor, with an open meaning
      scorer that anyone can rerun, document-level splits and cluster-bootstrap
      intervals. A human rating round showed that several of its first measures
      did not track what readers judged, so we dropped them and rebuilt it from
      measures that do (\autoref{sec:bench}, \autoref{sec:human}).
\item \textbf{A meaning judge.} A small judge, released in three public variants,
      that catches planted and real meaning errors at about 30 times the speed of
      its 31B teacher (\autoref{sec:judge}).
\item \textbf{An honest meaning/simplification trade-off.} Models that lower
      linguistic load change meaning in a measurable share of outputs; we report
      that share, and the changes (strength tag, corpus v1, \vThree{} and the app's
      code guards) that reduce it (\autoref{sec:modeling}, \autoref{sec:model3}).
\item \textbf{An on-device system}, with int8 encoder--decoder bundles decoded
      token by token, or with beam search, inside an Android app and measured on a phone
      (\autoref{sec:design}, \autoref{sec:ondevice}).
\end{itemize}

\autoref{sec:related} places the work in the literature. Section~2 describes the
data, the models, the system, the benchmark and the rating protocol; Section~3
reports the results for each research question; Section~4 draws the conclusions
and states the limitations.

\subsection{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\lead{Arabic simplification data.} SAMER \cite{samer} is the main human-written
multi-level Arabic simplification corpus, and it is small and narrow: 15 novels rewritten at two easier
levels, each version keeping the original's sentences by design. BAREC
\cite{barec} labels 69,441 sentences with 19 human reading levels but is not
parallel. Public LLM-written corpora provide several levels per source, and
DAASI \cite{daasi} human-verified rewrites of government and insurance text. No
corpus we found was written for readers with dyslexia, and none teaches sentence splitting: a
sentence is added in 0\% of SAMER pairs, 2--4\% of the LLM-written corpus's and 8\% of DAASI's
(\autoref{sec:eda}).

\lead{Controllable simplification.} A control token prepended to the source lets
one model rewrite for different audiences, first with target-grade tokens
\cite{scarton2018} and later with measured attributes such as length and lexical
complexity \cite{access,muss}. Our strength tags follow this line with one
difference: each tag names the corpus style it was learned from, so that a
meaning filter can be applied to exactly the sources that need one.

\lead{Evaluating simplification.} SARI \cite{sari} is the field's standard, but it
correlates only $r$ = 0.36 with human judgements of simplicity
\cite{alvamanchego2021}, and similarity-based metrics detect deleted content but
not inserted or substituted content \cite{devaraj2022}. Reference-free estimates
of the reading level track simplicity better \cite{cripwell2023}, and edit-level
error taxonomies track meaning \cite{salsa}. For benchmark design we follow
behavioural testing \cite{checklist}, construct-validity and reporting checklists
\cite{bean2025,betterbench}, and document-clustered intervals \cite{miller2024}.
LLM judges are known to favour outputs close to data they wrote or approved
\cite{li2026}, so an automatic judge must be checked against people before its
scores are trusted; we report agreement between raters with Krippendorff's
$\alpha$ \cite{krippendorff}.

\lead{The synthetic corpus, v0.} Our first generation pipeline took BAREC train
and dev sources, drew five candidates per source from DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, and
accepted one only if an LLM equivalence validator and the CAMeL BAREC readability
model both passed it. The export was split by a salted hash of the normalised
source, so that a sentence repeated under several BAREC identifiers never
straddles two splits; BAREC test was never used. The corpus was completed after
the v0.2 models had been trained, so no result in this report depends on it. Its data card
and the audit that followed list what was wrong with it: tashkeel in 56.5\% of
rows; 63.4\% of cut-off sources ``completed'' by the generator; 11.1\% of short
sources rewritten that should have been left alone; and only 42.9\% of pairs at
least two CAMeL levels easier. The equivalence validator, which scored 84\% on 25
annotated pairs, was weaker than that number suggests: when we planted errors in
its inputs it accepted 34\% of added sentences and 25\% of deleted ones at its
threshold of 0.7. We therefore did not train on v0, and we kept it online, tagged
\texttt{v0}, with these defects stated.

\lead{The synthetic corpus, v1.} Corpus v1 replaces it on the Hub's \texttt{main}
branch. Gemma~4 31B writes the targets from a stricter prompt, a calibrated Qwen judge
checks meaning at a threshold of 0.75, and a
target must lead its source by at least two readability levels. The 14,975 rows
have no violation of the rule checks, and an audit of 1,039 pairs found 1.7\% with
a changed meaning. It trains \vThree{}.

\subsection{Training Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\lead{Task.} Given a source sentence $x$ and a strength tag $t$, the model
produces a rewrite $\hat y = f(t \oplus x)$ that should keep the meaning of $x$ and
be easier to read. Training minimises token-level cross-entropy on pairs
$(t \oplus x, y)$; decoding is greedy, the setting the app runs for \vTwo{} and \vTwoFast{} (\vThree{}
decodes with beam search in the app, \autoref{sec:design}).

\lead{Base models.} Simplification is a sequence-to-sequence task, so every
candidate is a pretrained encoder--decoder transformer (\autoref{tab:basemodels}).
AraT5v2 came first because it beat AraBART at all three levels in the published
baselines of the corpus we add to the v0.2 training set \cite{baseet}; AraBART is the on-device candidate because it is 2.6
times smaller.

\lead{Input normalisation.} No vocabulary in \autoref{tab:basemodels} was built
for diacritized text: in AraT5v2 only 1.6\% of pieces carry a diacritic, and
AraBART cannot represent one at all. Every model therefore receives its source
with tashkeel and tatweel removed, in training and in the app; \autoref{sec:modeling}
measures what happens otherwise.

\lead{\vOne{}.} We fine-tuned AraT5v2 to rewrite SAMER's level-5 sentences into
their level-3 simplifications with the prefix \ar{بسّط:}\ (\emph{simplify:}):
AdamW at learning rate $10^{-4}$ with weight decay 0.01 and 100 warm-up steps, an
effective batch of 16, mixed precision, 10 epochs (3,250 steps) and seed 42.
Pairs whose simplification equals the source (42\% of SAMER train) were capped at
20\% of training, and the checkpoint with the best SARI on a 500-row dev subset
was kept.

\lead{\vTwo{} and \vTwoFast{}.} The diagnosis of \vOne{} (\autoref{sec:modeling}) identified three
problems, and the v0.2 recipe changes one thing for each:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
\item \emph{Inaction was rewarded for the wrong reason.} Capping unchanged pairs
      at 20\% while choosing the checkpoint on a dev sample that is 43\% unchanged
      pulls the model both ways. The v0.2 recipe keeps all 6,033 unchanged SAMER pairs,
      so it learns when not to edit, and selects its checkpoint on SARI over dev
      rows a human changed.
\item \emph{SAMER is novels, and it teaches word substitution.} The v0.2 recipe adds
      DAASI's everyday and administrative text and an external corpus of
      educational text. The external targets are LLM-written, and many are
      summaries that drop facts, delete answer options or invent a speaker. An
      external pair is kept only if it
      passes a \emph{meaning filter}: LaBSE similarity $\geq$ 0.646, a target at
      least 67\% of the source's length, and every number kept. Each threshold is
      the 5th percentile of the same measure over DAASI's human-verified
      rewrites, so a pair is rejected only when it departs from its source more
      than 95\% of human simplifications do.
\item \emph{One strength does not fit every reader.} Every source carries a tag
      naming the style it was learned from (\autoref{tab:m2data}). At run time the
      tag is the reader's strength setting, so one model serves light, medium and
      strong rewrites \cite{scarton2018,access}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{System Design}
\label{sec:design}

\autoref{fig:arch} shows the system as tested on a phone. The Android app
registers an activity for \texttt{android.\allowbreak intent.\allowbreak action.\allowbreak PROCESS\_TEXT}
(\texttt{text/plain}, Android 6.0 and later), so \ar{تبسيط} appears in the
selection menu of any app. A translucent activity draws the result over the host
app without the overlay permission; an optional floating panel, which does
require it, keeps reading while the reader returns to the page.

\lead{Inference.} The simplifier ships as an int8 ONNX bundle (encoder, decoder
with a key--value cache, and tokenizer), downloaded once, verified by SHA-256, and
run by ONNX Runtime with a decoding loop written in Kotlin that blocks repeated
3-grams and stops at 256 tokens. \vTwoFast{} (the fast model) and \vTwo{} decode greedily and
stream each token to the screen as it is produced. \vThree{}, the large model, decodes
with four beams (the setting \emph{More faithful, slower}, on by default; \#61, \#62,
\#66) and shows each sentence when it is finished: a port
of transformers' beam search that runs the beams as one batch and reorders the
decoder's cache when a beam continues another.
On the same CPU the app's engine gives transformers' exact tokens on 120 of 120
BayanBench sentences, and the search matches transformers' own on all 578 test
cases whose decisions are clear of float rounding. Beams can still change earlier words until they finish, so with
the setting on each sentence is shown when it is final. The app strips tashkeel, splits the
selection into sentences and decodes them one by one. Sentences under six words (headings, labels) pass
through unchanged, because the model was trained on longer sentences and invents
text around fragments.

\lead{The app's text step.} Before the model, the selection is cleaned (PDF line
breaks, presentation and Persian letter forms, digits glued to letters, list
bullets), Arabic-Indic and Persian digits are mapped to Western digits, and
scripture and set poetry are detected (Qur'anic marks and verse brackets, the
usual quotation formulas, fully vowelled verse) and \emph{never sent to the
model}: they come back exactly as selected. After the model, a sentence keeps its
source wording when the rewrite keeps less than 35\% of its content words, drops
or changes a number, drops a Latin-script word, or changes the count of negations
or of limits (\emph{at least}, \emph{only}, \dots); since \#66 it also checks
condition words and counts Latin-script words one by one, the checks \vThree{}'s
benchmarked safety net used (\#61). Digits return in the source's
own style, and a selection cut mid-sentence keeps its open ending. On the
BayanBench dev core items these guards raise numbers kept from 72\% to 100\% and
protected text and cut-off endings to 100\%, remove every flagged negation and
limit change, and raise meaning kept by about 3.5 points without changing how
much is simplified; they fall back on 3--7\% of sentences. The original is always
one tap away.

\lead{Read-aloud and diacritization.} Voices run on the device through
sherpa-onnx. The default, Nabra-7M-Distill, is a Kokoro-architecture Arabic
voice exported to int8 (8.7\,MB); three Piper voices are optional downloads. The
spoken word is highlighted as it is read. The diacritizer, Libtashkeel
(\autoref{tab:diac}), runs on the device too: a 4.8\,MB character model bundled
with the app. With \emph{Show tashkeel} on (off by default, since full vowels can
crowd a line), each finished sentence gets its short vowels after the guards, so
the reader, read-aloud, copy and history all use the same text. The Kotlin port
gives the Python package's exact output on 305 BayanBench texts and never changes a
letter.

\subsection{BayanBench}
\label{sec:bench}

SARI measures agreement with one editor's rewrite. A reader with dyslexia needs
something else: that a selection comes back \emph{easier to read} and \emph{with
the same meaning}, and that what should not change does not. No public benchmark
tests this for Arabic, so we built one against the construct-validity checklist
of \cite{bean2025} and the reporting practices of \cite{betterbench}.

\lead{Construct.} A rewrite is dyslexia-friendly when it says the same thing in a
form that is easier to read and follow: shorter clauses, more familiar words,
no new errors, and restraint where the text is already easy. Visual formatting, read-aloud and
tashkeel are out of scope, and summarising is always a failure: dropping content
never counts as simplification.

\lead{Measures.} Every output is scored on separate measures, grouped in blocks
and never combined into one number (\autoref{tab:tracks}); the probe tracks follow
CheckList \cite{checklist}.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Meaning}, from an open model (below): the mean P(same meaning), and
      \emph{meaning kept}, an output whose P(same meaning) is at least 0.5 and
      that keeps every number of its source. An unchanged copy keeps its meaning
      by definition.
\item \emph{Simpler}, as changes from source to output, each on the items where
      there is something to simplify: words cut from the longest clause (sources
      with a clause over 15 words, BAREC's guideline limit for level 10, about
      grade 4), reading levels lowered (the maximum sentence level predicted by the
      CAMeL BAREC readability model, sources at level 10 or above), and hard words
      removed (words at levels 4--5 of the SAMER readability lexicon).
\item \emph{Checks}, deterministic: every number kept, and no house punctuation
      rule broken.
\item \emph{Behaviour}: easy text left unchanged (C), protected text keeping its
      letters (F), and a cut-off selection keeping its ending (E1).
\item \emph{Flags}: a changed count of negations, limits (at least, at most,
      only) or conditions. A flag marks an output for attention and is never a
      failure on its own, because a rewrite can rephrase a negation correctly.
\end{itemize}
A \emph{clause} is a maximal span between punctuation marks (full stop, question
and exclamation marks, comma, semicolon, colon, line break), so the clause
measure credits added commas as well as split sentences and is read next to
meaning. Numbers are compared as multisets after mapping Arabic-Indic and Persian
digits to Western ones; negations are counted over eleven negating particles. The
pass/fail measures of the first version (no clause over 15 words, at least 60\% of
the source's words kept) are still computed as diagnostics.

\lead{Items and splits.} The 1,981 items form four sets that are reported side by
side and never averaged. \emph{Core} items come from BAREC test documents.
\emph{Held-out} items are news and legal text, including 247 BAREC sentences of
those domains that no model trained on. \emph{Outside} items are 344 sentences
from UN News, WHO fact sheets, newly created Arabic Wikipedia articles, UNHCR
help pages and Android help, fetched on 28 September and therefore published
after every corpus we use. \emph{Written} items are 50 medicine-leaflet sentences
written for the benchmark by an LLM, kept out of core because they are not real
text. Items are split by document (731 dev items from 129 documents, 1,250 test
items from 197), so no document contributes to both splits. Any BAREC document
with a sentence found in any training or generated data was dropped whole (17
documents), and near-copies were removed by word-trigram overlap.

\lead{The meaning scorer.} Meaning is scored by Gemma~4 31B \cite{gemma4}, run in
its 4-bit quantisation-aware checkpoint with vLLM \cite{vllm}, as a scorer rather
than a writer: it sees the source and the output, never the system's name, and
answers four yes/no questions (does the rewrite keep exactly the same meaning;
does it add a fact; does it drop a fact or qualifier; does it contradict the
source). The probability of \emph{yes} is read from one forward pass, so no text
is generated and the score is continuous. Scores are shared data keyed by the
content of the (source, output) pair, so scoring a system needs no GPU, an output
nobody has scored is reported as pending rather than guessed, and a meaning rate
is shown only once 90\% of its items are scored. The first version of the
benchmark used pass/fail verdicts from a closed model, Gemini~3.1~Pro, with a
fixed checklist; the open scorer replaces it because anyone can rerun it, it
costs about \$0.12 for 2,700 pairs, and it gives a graded score whose threshold
can be checked against people. The 0.5 threshold was set before the human
ratings and kept after them (\autoref{sec:human}). The v0.2 training data
contains nothing a Gemma model wrote or approved, and the written items come from
a different model family, which limits preference leakage \cite{li2026}.

\lead{Statistics and release.} Every rate and mean carries a 95\% interval from a
bootstrap over whole documents (2,000 resamples), because sentences from one
document pass or fail together \cite{miller2024}; a comparison counts only when
the interval of the paired difference excludes zero. The benchmark ships as a
Python package whose command-line tool covers the whole cycle (\texttt{verify},
\texttt{predict}, \texttt{score}, \texttt{meaning-pending}, \texttt{meaning}),
with its data, model outputs and meaning scores in a checksum-pinned Hugging Face
dataset, so any team member can score a model on a laptop, on Kaggle or on Linux.

\subsection{Human Rating Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

An automatic scorer is only useful if it agrees with people, so we rated a sample
of model outputs before trusting it. The rating set holds \hSingles{} single
outputs and \hPairs{} side-by-side comparisons from the dev split (core and outside
items, \hDocs{} documents), drawn from the six v0.2 systems of
\autoref{tab:benchhead}. Copies of the input were not rated, because there is
nothing to judge, and protected and constructed items (F, E1, E2) were left out.
\hEven{} outputs were drawn evenly, 35 per system; \hNear{} more were drawn where the
scorer was least sure (P(same) between 0.1 and 0.9, the narrowest band that held
enough candidates), so the check does not rest only on easy cases. \hShared{}
outputs and 15 comparisons went to every rater, to measure agreement; the rest
were split one rating each.

For each output a rater saw the source and the rewrite, without the system's
name, and answered three questions: \emph{meaning} (same, a minor change, a major
change), \emph{Arabic} (correct, a minor error, a major error) and \emph{ease} for a
reader with dyslexia (easier, the same, harder). For a comparison the question was
which version is better for such a reader: easier, with the same meaning. Five
team members rated on a web page built for the round, which saved every answer
as it was given and bound each rater to one name. A sixth rater, a reader with
dyslexia, gave informed consent and answered only the ease question, worded for
them as \mbox{\ar{هل كانت أسهل عليك؟}} (was it easier for you?), on 30 shared
outputs, and for a comparison \mbox{\ar{أي النسختين كانت أسهل عليك في القراءة؟}}
(which version was easier for you to read?), on 10. An output's meaning counts as kept when a strict majority of its
raters said \emph{same}; a tie counts as not kept.

\subsection{The Meaning Judge}
\label{sec:judge}

Meaning is the property a simplifier most easily breaks and the one no
reference-based score sees, so we trained a judge for it. It is a four-head
Gemma~4 E2B model distilled from the 31B teacher that scores a (source, output)
pair, released on the Hugging Face Hub in three variants (bf16, w8a8 and w4a16;
CC BY-NC). It replaces the Gemini judge of BayanBench v1, whose verdicts had been
validated only against themselves.

\lead{What it catches.} At a 5\% false-reject rate the bf16 judge flags 95.5\% of
planted errors and 86.5\% of changes that humans labelled as meaning changes. On
subtle real errors, those a rater finds only on a careful second reading, it flags
about 62\%. It reaches about 94\% of the teacher's score at about 30 times the
speed. The judge is not a substitute for a reader: it screens
corpora and checkpoints, and the 31B model remains the reference scorer in
BayanBench v2.

\lead{The human rating round.} To check any judge against people, we collected
ratings of 245 single outputs on three questions: whether the meaning is kept,
whether the Arabic is good, and whether the text is easier. Agreement was
Krippendorff's $\alpha$ = 0.51 for meaning, 0.17 for Arabic quality and 0.19 for
ease. Only meaning is firm enough to validate a judge against, so the judge was
validated on meaning alone. One reader with dyslexia rated ease only. The round is
not a reader study: it is a small set of ratings that told us which measures to
trust, and no reader has yet used the application (objective~O4).

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}
\label{sec:prep}

\lead{Conversion.} SAMER's aligned levels were converted to source--target pairs
(level~5 $\rightarrow$ level~3). One converter turns each locked test file into
the scoring format, keeps every row (including the 1,599 SAMER test rows, 48.8\%,

\lead{Normalisation for comparison.} For leakage checks and scoring alignment,
diacritics and punctuation are removed and the alef, \ar{ة}/\ar{ه} and
\ar{ى}/\ar{ي} variants are unified. Training text keeps its spelling apart from
tashkeel and tatweel.

\autoref{fig:cleaning} shows the pipeline that produced the current corpus. Text
is cleaned first, then each source is routed: scripture, poetry and other
protected text, and headings of five words or fewer, are copied unchanged; only
the remaining sources are rewritten, and a rewrite must pass four gates before it
enters the corpus.

\subsection{Modeling}
\label{sec:modeling}

\lead{Setup.} Reference-based scores use SARI \cite{sari} through the
\texttt{evaluate} implementation, beside BLEU, BERTScore between source and output
\cite{bertscore}, and the fall in predicted BAREC level. Every score is paired
with the copy baseline and split into rows a human changed and rows a human left
unchanged. Each final model was run once per test set after training and
selection were complete.

\lead{RQ1: the reference-based view.} Because half of SAMER needs no change,
copying scores SARI \copytestall{} on the full test set but only \copytestchanged{}
on the \testchangedn{} rows a human changed, and the second number is the bar a
model must clear. \autoref{tab:test} and \autoref{fig:sari} report \vOne{}, with
the harness validated first: it reproduced the training run's 2,983 dev
predictions row for row.

\subsection{Human Evaluation}
\label{sec:human}

\lead{Agreement between raters.} On the \hShared{} outputs given to every rater, the
raters agreed moderately on meaning (Krippendorff's $\alpha$ = \hAlphaMeaning{},
interval metric; \hExactMeaning\% of rater pairs gave the same answer) and weakly on
Arabic ($\alpha$ = \hAlphaArabic{}) and ease ($\alpha$ = \hAlphaEase{}). The Arabic
figure is low partly because almost every output was rated correct, so the raters
agreed on \hExactArabic\% of pairs yet the few errors they flagged rarely
coincided. Meaning is therefore the one human judgement firm enough to check an
automatic scorer against; ease is reported per system but not used as a target.

\lead{The meaning scorer against people.} \autoref{tab:scorerauc} and
\autoref{fig:scorer} compare the scorer's P(same meaning) with the raters'
verdict on all \hRated{} rated outputs. It separates the outputs the raters kept
from the rest with an AUC of \hAucSame{} [\hAucSameLo--\hAucSameHi], rising to
\hAucThree{} on the \hThreeN{} outputs with three or more raters, where the human
verdict is itself more reliable; it is \hAucNotMajor{} when a minor change counts as
kept. Its scores are bimodal, so the cut-off matters less than the ranking. As a
pass/fail rule at 0.5 it catches \hCaught{} of the \hLosses{} outputs the raters
judged changed, including all \hMajorCaught{} with a major change, and fails
\hWrongFail{} of the \hKept{} that the raters kept: the scorer is stricter than
people, so ``meaning kept'' is a conservative rate. A cut-off of 0.3 would have
failed seven fewer kept outputs and caught the same losses; we kept the 0.5 set
before the ratings rather than tune the threshold on the sample used to check it.

\label{sec:benchres}

\lead{RQ2: the v0.2 models on BayanBench.} Each of the seven systems was run once on the
BayanBench splits: the v0.2 models on both architectures in the medium setting
(\texttt{[S2]}) and without a tag, the two int8 bundles exactly as the app calls
them, and copying the input. \autoref{tab:benchhead} reports the \benchheadsplit{}
split on core items; \autoref{tab:benchfx} isolates one change at a time, and
\autoref{tab:benchother} gives the other split. Five findings follow.

\benchheadtable

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{F\arabic*}, leftmargin=8mm]
\item \textbf{Meaning and simplification trade off.} The medium setting cuts
      \bvHTfiveClause{} (AraT5v2) and \bvHBartClause{} (AraBART) words from the
      longest clause and keeps the meaning in \bvHTfiveKept\% and \bvHBartKept\%
      of outputs. Without the tag the same models cut \bvHTfiveNClause{} and
      \bvHBartNClause{} words and keep \bvHTfiveNKept\% and \bvHBartNKept\%; the
      int8 AraT5v2 bundle cuts \bvHAppTfiveClause{} and keeps \bvHAppTfiveKept\%
      (\autoref{fig:tradeoff}). Within each architecture, the tagged setting
      simplifies most and keeps meaning least often, and no system simplifies as
      much as the tagged setting while keeping meaning as often as the shipped
      bundle.
\item \textbf{AraT5v2 keeps meaning better at every setting.} At the same setting
      AraT5v2 keeps the meaning \bfxHTfiveNvsBartNKept{} points more often than
      AraBART without a tag \bfxHTfiveNvsBartNKeptCI, \bfxHTfivevsBartKept{} with
      the \texttt{[S2]} tag and \bfxHAppTfivevsAppBartKept{} as an int8 bundle,
      every interval excluding zero; without a tag the two cut long clauses by
      similar amounts. The human ratings agree (\autoref{fig:dumbbell}).
\item \textbf{Simplification is mostly structural.} The predicted reading level
      falls by at most \bvHmaxLevel{} levels on average, and each output removes
      \bvHminHard--\bvHmaxHard{} hard words on average, where a clause loses up to
      \bvHmaxClause{} words: the models shorten clauses far more than they change
      vocabulary.
\item \textbf{Restraint is weak.} Easy text comes back unchanged in only
      \bvHminC--\bvHmaxC\% of C items, protected text keeps its letters in
      \bvHminF--\bvHmaxF\%, and a selection cut mid-sentence keeps its ending
      in \bvHminEone--\bvHmaxEone\% of E1 items; the rest are completed, sometimes
      with invented words (\autoref{tab:errors}). No training corpus shows these
      behaviours: SAMER's unchanged pairs are literary sentences, and no corpus
      contains protected or cut-off text. Numbers survive in
      \bvHminNum--\bvHmaxNum\% of the core items that contain one. The app's
      text step now handles protected text, numbers and cut-off endings in code
      (\autoref{sec:design}), and \vThree{} improves restraint (\autoref{sec:model3}).
\item \textbf{The deployed format buys meaning with ease.} The bundles the app
      ships send no strength tag (\autoref{tab:benchfx}). Dropping the tag raises
      meaning kept by \bfxHTfiveNvsTfiveKept{} (AraT5v2) and \bfxHBartNvsBartKept{}
      (AraBART) points and cuts \bfxHTfiveNvsTfiveClause{} and
      \bfxHBartNvsBartClause{} fewer words from the longest clause. Quantising to
      int8 rewords most outputs (the app's AraT5v2 outputs match the float
      model's on only 234 of 731 dev items). For AraBART it makes no measurable
      difference to meaning kept. For AraT5v2 it moves the model toward copying
      (\bfxHAppTfivevsTfiveNClause{} fewer words cut, \bfxHAppTfivevsTfiveNC{}
      points more easy text left alone) and keeps the meaning
      \bfxHAppTfivevsTfiveNKept{} points more often \bfxHAppTfivevsTfiveNKeptCI{}
      on this split, though not on dev, which makes the shipped AraT5v2 bundle the
      safest system and the least simplifying one.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Testing and Improvements}

\lead{Testing.} The first plugin shell shipped 41 unit and Robolectric tests. The
app tested on the phone unit-tests its text pipeline (sentence splitting,
punctuation repair, clitic normalisation and the retention fallback), and its
download pipeline checks every file by SHA-256. The leakage checker is tested against a
planted duplicate and must exit non-zero. The benchmark package has 12 tests on
a built-in toy dataset, and its prediction command reproduces our v0.2
outputs on all 731 dev items, as an independent rerun by a teammate confirmed.

faults: the scoring script put every BAREC row in the ``unchanged'' bucket; the
shared prediction script omitted \vOne{}'s training prefix, which alone cost

AraT5v2's tied embeddings and AraBART's sentence markers are handled
differently. Each now has a guard; the last makes the prediction command refuse
to run under version~5.

\subsection{On the Device}
\label{sec:ondevice}

\lead{RQ3: cost on the phone.} Both simplifiers were exported to ONNX with the
tokenizer inside the bundle and quantised to int8 (\autoref{tab:device}). The
AraBART bundle, the default, fits the 250\,MB budget. The AraT5v2 bundles are
optional downloads of 471\,MB: 46\% of their weights are two untied 110k-row
embedding matrices, and we kept them whole rather than trade quality for size. We tested both bundles
in the app on a Xiaomi Mi~11X (Snapdragon 870, Android 13, 6\,GB RAM), a phone that
now sits in the mid-range, with the same two-sentence paragraph and six cold starts
each. Because output streams, the reader waits for the first token, not the whole
sentence: with AraBART 82--172\,ms for a paragraph's first sentence and 28--30\,ms
for each later one, with AraT5v2 177--318\,ms and 55--85\,ms. AraBART loads in
1.0--1.7\,s against 2.6--7.3\,s, and finishes a sentence about twice as fast, the
same ratio as on the laptop. \vThree{} has the same architecture and bundle size as
the AraT5v2 bundle measured here. With the beam setting on it decodes with four
beams, which runs the decoder on four rows at a time and gives up streaming: over 120 BayanBench dev
sentences it takes 1.19\,s per sentence (median; 90th percentile 2.43\,s) on the
phone, against 0.41\,s when the same bundle decodes greedily. Quantisation is not neutral for AraT5v2: int8
changed 62--74\% of its outputs against the float model, though not the rate at
which meaning is kept (F5). We did not prune their vocabulary, since any change to
the shipped weights would need the whole evaluation again; the bundles are 449\,MiB and 212\,MiB on disk, the
471\,MB and 222\,MB of \autoref{tab:device} in decimal units.

The answers to the research questions are these. \textbf{RQ1:} no. On copy-heavy
test sets, SARI rewards inaction and is blind to reversals of meaning; a model
that beats copying on the sentences editors changed still scores below copying
overall, and what it learned from SAMER is vocabulary substitution without
restructuring. An open meaning scorer, by contrast, agrees with human raters (AUC
\hAucSame{}) and can be rerun by anyone. \textbf{RQ2:} partly. The models lower
linguistic load, but not for free: every setting trades meaning for
simplification, the safest one simplifies least, and AraT5v2 keeps the meaning
of \bvHTfiveKept--\bvHAppTfiveKept\% of outputs, more than AraBART. Removing the
strength tag helps meaning, so the untagged bundles the app ships are safer than
the tagged settings. \vThree{} with four beams moves the trade-off: it keeps the meaning more often than
any earlier model and simplifies more than \vTwo{} in the app. \textbf{RQ3:} yes for AraBART, in part for AraT5v2. Streamed output
reaches the reader in under a third of a second on a mid-range phone with either
bundle (\vThree{} with the beam setting on shows a whole sentence in about
1.2\,s); the default int8 AraBART bundle fits the 250\,MB budget, the optional
AraT5v2 bundles, \vThree{}'s included, take 471\,MB, and int8 changes most of
AraT5v2's outputs against the float model.

\lead{Benefits.} For readers: a tool that offers a simpler version where and when
the difficulty occurs, offline and private, with the original always one tap
away; we have not yet tested whether it helps readers with dyslexia. For schools:
a tool that runs on the phones students already own. For the field: a documented
case of test-set contamination in a public Arabic simplification corpus, an
evaluation protocol that exposes copy-heavy scores, and a benchmark and a meaning
judge, checked against people, that score behaviours rather than agreement with
one editor.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Future Improvements}
\label{sec:future}

The evaluation points to specific next steps. The central one is to move the
trade-off of F1: a model that simplifies as much as the tagged setting while
keeping meaning as often as the shipped bundle. The meaning scorer, now checked
against people, can rank candidate rewrites and select checkpoints, so that
training is steered by the behaviours readers need rather than by SARI. \vThree{} with four
beams is the first step: it keeps \vTwo{}'s meaning rate in the app while cutting
\mBeamTClause{} words from the longest clause, short of the tagged setting's
\bvHTfiveClause{}. Making that quality the default needs faster beam decoding on the
phone, since four beams take about three times as long per sentence as greedy. The AraT5v2 bundles could be made
smaller by pruning their vocabulary to the pieces Arabic text uses (46\% of their
parameters sit in two embedding matrices; about 223\,MB pruned), but only if the
full evaluation shows no loss. Sentence splitting needs
training data that teaches it, which no human-written corpus provides. Last, a
reading study with more readers with dyslexia, measuring reading time and
comprehension, would turn ratings into evidence about reading.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{One reader with dyslexia.} The reader rated rewrites on a web page;
      nobody with dyslexia has yet used the app or been measured reading with it,
      and the five team raters do not have dyslexia.
\item \textbf{The meaning scorer is a model.} It agrees with people at AUC
      \hAucSame{} but is stricter than them, failing \hWrongFail{} of the \hKept{}
      outputs they kept, so ``meaning kept'' is a conservative rate. It was
      checked on dev outputs of the six v0.2 systems, from \hDocs{} documents; a
      very different system could be judged differently.
\item \textbf{Ease and Arabic are weakly agreed.} Raters agreed on ease and on
      correct Arabic with $\alpha$ of only \hAlphaEase{} and \hAlphaArabic{}, so
      the benchmark measures ease through clause length, reading level and hard
      words, and checks Arabic only through house rules.
\item \textbf{The clause measure credits commas.} A rewrite can shorten its
      longest clause by adding commas as well as by splitting sentences, which is
      why it is always read next to meaning.
\item \textbf{No reference-based test score for the v0.2 models.} They are compared
      with published work only through its dev selection score; its test
      evaluation is BayanBench, which has no human references.
\item \textbf{Some tracks are small.} E3 and E4 hold 64 and 40 test items, and
      few core items contain a number (\autoref{tab:benchhead}), so these rates separate only large
      differences.
\item \textbf{One phone.} On-device figures come from one mid-range phone; the
      optional AraT5v2 bundles (471\,MB) need more storage than the default.
\item \textbf{\vThree{}'s meaning rests on the judge.} With four beams every test
      core item is scored, but no significant difference is not proof of none: the
      interval on test runs from \mBeamTKeptCi{} points. The human rating of
      \vThree{} covers 18 sentences.
\item \textbf{One run per configuration.} Each model was trained once, with one
      seed, so the comparison between architectures carries no estimate of
      training variance.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Conclusion}

Bayan shows that Arabic text can be simplified entirely on a mid-range phone,
from inside any app, at interactive latency, and that the reduction in linguistic
load can be measured honestly. Getting there
depended less on model size than on evaluation. The field's standard score hid
both what a model had learned and what it broke. And the behaviours a reader needs --- shorter clauses, familiar words,
the same meaning, and restraint where the text is already easy --- had to be
measured one by one before they could be improved. BayanBench makes those
measurements reproducible by anyone on the team, its meaning scorer agrees with
human raters, and its results set the next goal plainly: simplify as much as the
boldest setting while keeping meaning as well as the safest.
