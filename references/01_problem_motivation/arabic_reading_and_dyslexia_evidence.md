## 2. Guardrail for the pitch: dyslexia is not learning poverty

Dyslexia prevalence (~11%, clinical/screened) and low reading proficiency (e.g. PISA below Level 2, or
the World Bank's "learning poverty") measure different things. Never present the large proficiency
figures as dyslexia rates. The defensible framing: Bayan targets readers with dyslexia; the same
barriers (unfamiliar vocabulary, long compound sentences, dense morphology) also affect the much larger
group of struggling readers.

## 3. Evidence that bears on simplification

These are the verified findings most relevant to *why simplifying vocabulary, syntax and morphology
should help* — Bayan's core problem.

### Dyslexia-specific evidence on simplification (other languages)

The strongest direct evidence that lexical simplification helps readers with dyslexia comes from Spanish
user studies — no Arabic equivalent was found.

| Finding | Source | Implication for Bayan |
|---|---|---|
| Eye-tracking study (46 participants: 23 with dyslexia, 23 controls): **more frequent words** made readers with dyslexia read significantly faster; **shorter words** improved their comprehension; no significant effects for controls | Rello, Baeza-Yates, Dempere-Marco & Saggion (2013), INTERACT. [doi:10.1007/978-3-642-40498-6_15](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-40498-6_15) | Word frequency and length are the lexical targets to optimize. |
| User study (96 participants: 47 with dyslexia, 49 controls): presenting **several simpler synonyms on request** was rated significantly more readable and comprehensible by participants with dyslexia than **automatically replacing** a complex word | Rello, Baeza-Yates, Bott & Saggion (2013), W4A. [doi:10.1145/2461121.2461126](https://doi.org/10.1145/2461121.2461126) | Keep the reader in control: show the original, offer simplifications or synonyms on demand, rather than silently rewriting. |
| Three language models evaluated against the British Dyslexia Association Style Guide (DysText metric): their knowledge of dyslexia-friendly writing was limited and their output should not be trusted without verification | "Dyslexia and AI: Do Language Models Align with Dyslexic Style Guide Criteria?", AIED 2025, [doi:10.1007/978-3-031-98414-3_3](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-98414-3_3) | Don't assume an LLM "knows" dyslexia-friendly Arabic; encode the rules explicitly. |
| Text simplification *technology* and its use in *assistive* systems raise distinct ethical questions and should be framed separately | Gooding (2022). [arXiv:2204.09565](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.09565) | Frame Bayan's claims carefully (who it helps, what it changes, the risk of altered meaning). |

Participant counts for the two Rello studies are from the papers' abstracts as indexed; the DOIs were
checked in Crossref.

### Landscape note (for the pitch)
Arabic diacritization and read-aloud with word highlighting already exist commercially — for example
the iOS app "Tashkeel — Arabic Literacy AI" (App Store, 2026) — but it neither simplifies text nor
targets dyslexia. Microsoft's Immersive Reader does not support its text-spacing feature for Arabic.
Bayan's differentiator is **simplification for dyslexic readers**, not the auxiliary features.
